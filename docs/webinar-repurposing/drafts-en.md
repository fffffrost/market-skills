## A1 — Owned blog article

**Reader question:** How can a small B2B marketing team hand competitor research to product and sales?

**Title:** Before comparing competitors, make the evidence comparable

**Sources:** P01–P13, P15. **Owner:** Content lead; research reviewer. **Suggested day:** D1. **Status:** Automatically checked; not human-reviewed.

**Draft — fictional teaching example**

A competitor spreadsheet can contain plenty of information without supporting a decision. In this fictional scenario, a small B2B marketing team has collected screenshots, quotes and user comments. Before handing the work to product and sales, it needs to explain which claims are comparable, which are supported, and which still need checking.

Start by naming the decision. If the task is to choose options for a product demonstration, the report does not need to rank the entire market. Write down the job, required conditions, exclusions and decision owner. Keep the conclusion within that scope. A recommendation to investigate an option is not a purchase verdict.

Product labels are not enough to define competitors. A service that turns a transcript into written drafts and one that edits short videos may both describe themselves as content platforms. They address different jobs. Separate direct alternatives, adjacent options and manual workarounds, and explain why each belongs in the comparison.

### Check the comparison conditions

The teaching source proposes four checks: the user and job; the plan and feature boundaries; the pricing unit, payment period and currency; and the date, region and origin of the evidence. Mark missing conditions rather than silently filling them in.

The fictional Alpha card describes per-seat billing with an annually paid monthly equivalent. Beta is billed per project and paid monthly. Neither card specifies usage volume, tax, region or additional service charges. The supported conclusion is that the billing units and periods differ and comparable quotes require a shared usage assumption. The cards do not establish which option costs less.

Broad questions also need to become testable. Instead of asking whether collaboration is good, ask whether editors and reviewers have different permissions, whether revision history exists, and whether the required files can be exported. Current documentation, a demonstration or a provider response can help answer those questions. The research table does not replace a test that has not happened.

### Connect each conclusion to a specific claim

A saved link tells the next person where to look. It does not explain which sentence supports the conclusion, which plan it concerns or what was visible when the research was done.

This teaching worksheet uses nine fields: evidence ID, verification question, product and plan, source location, short excerpt or accurate paraphrase, access date, supported scope, current status, and research owner. In this example, source locations are P paragraph IDs because the source contains no actual external research. Do not invent vendor URLs or access records.

The fictional E01 entry asks whether billing units can be compared. Its source is P05 and its paraphrase is “Alpha is per seat; Beta is per project.” Its scope is limited to those two teaching cards, and usage assumptions are still missing. E01 cannot establish the lower-cost product. Likewise, evidence of draft export cannot establish automatic publication to every platform.

### Preserve conflicts and unknowns

The source uses four worksheet states: supported, unverified, conflicting, and not applicable. These are local teaching conventions, not an industry standard. Even a supported statement still needs its date and scope.

When a current page and an older quote disagree, keep both. Record differences in date, plan, region and terms. Do not assume the newer page overrides every older contract. Specify which conditions matter to the present decision and what needs confirmation. Change the conclusion when confirmation arrives and preserve the reason for that change.

Reviews can suggest questions, but one review does not establish how common an experience is. Turn a complaint into a testable question and look for documentation or demonstration evidence for the relevant version. A feature missing from a review is not necessarily missing from the product.

If no supporting statement has been found, write: “Within the materials checked, we have not found documentation supporting this capability; further verification is needed.” Then identify the material categories searched, missing evidence and person responsible for follow-up. If the capability affects the demonstration shortlist, make it a required verification item rather than an unsupported negative verdict.

### Hand over three things

Give the next person a decision summary, an evidence table and a verification checklist. The summary sets the scope, supported judgments and important unknowns. The table makes the evidence traceable. The checklist assigns questions, role owners and the material still needed. Where a provider response is required, include a confirmation deadline without pretending a response has arrived.

This fictional exercise illustrates a research handoff, not a real procurement decision or customer outcome. ## A4 — Short post: missing information

**Reader question:** How should a report describe a capability it cannot verify?

**Title:** “Not found” is not a feature verdict

**Sources:** P06, P09, P11, P12, P15. **Owner:** Content lead; research reviewer. **Suggested day:** D5. **Status:** Automatically checked; not human-reviewed.

**Draft — fictional teaching example**

A feature is not necessarily absent because the material you checked does not mention it. In this example, use a narrower statement: “Within the materials checked, we have not found documentation supporting this capability; further verification is needed.”

Add the material categories searched, evidence still required, and role responsible for checking. If the capability determines whether an option reaches the next demonstration, put it on the verification checklist.

Reviews follow the same boundary. A missing mention does not establish absence; a complaint is a question to investigate, not proof that everyone has the same problem.

## A6 — FAQ

**Reader question:** What should be decided before handing the research over?

**Title:** Seven questions about a defensible competitor handoff

**Sources:** P02–P04, P07, P09–P13, P15. **Owner:** Content lead; research reviewer. **Suggested day:** D6. **Status:** Automatically checked; not human-reviewed.

**Draft — fictional teaching example**

**1. How wide should the comparison be?**
Define the decision first. A shortlist for demonstrations needs the job, required conditions, exclusions and decision owner. Distinguish direct, adjacent and manual alternatives rather than assuming similarly named products do the same work. (P02, P04)

**2. When does evidence expire?**
The source gives no universal number of days. Keep the actual access date and applicable scope, and recheck evidence when the conditions relevant to the decision change. Do not invent a standard expiry period. (P03, P07, P09, P10)

**3. What if two sources conflict?**
Keep both, including differences in date, plan, region and terms. Determine which conditions matter to this decision, assign the confirmation question and update the conclusion only when new evidence arrives. Preserve the reason for the change. (P10)

**4. Can a user review establish a product fact?**
A review can suggest a question to test. A single experience does not establish prevalence, and a missing mention does not establish that a capability is absent. Look for relevant documentation or demonstration evidence. (P11)

**5. How should missing feature information be written?**
Say that the checked material does not establish the capability and further verification is required. List what was checked, what is missing and who will follow up. Do not replace that uncertainty with “unsupported” or “impossible.” (P12)

**6. Which evidence states are used here?**
Supported, unverified, conflicting and not applicable. These are the teaching worksheet's conventions, not an industry standard; supported evidence still needs a date and scope. (P09)

**7. What does the receiving team actually get?**
A short decision summary, an evidence table and a verification checklist. A suggested demonstration remains a proposed action until it has happened; it must not be written as a completed acceptance test. (P13)
