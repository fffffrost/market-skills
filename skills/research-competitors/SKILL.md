# Research Competitors

Build a decision-ready competitor landscape without treating every similar product as a direct competitor.

## Choose the depth

- Use a **quick scan** to classify a named set and flag omissions.
- Use a **full landscape** to discover the market, build layers, compare priorities, and derive implications.
- Use a **deep dive** for hands-on validation of a few priority products.
- Use an **update pass** to check changes since a dated snapshot without rewriting stable conclusions.

Read [references/research-template.md](references/research-template.md) for a full landscape or formal report.

## Follow the workflow

### 1. Frame the decision

Record the research object, target reader, decision to support, geography, time boundary, expected deliverable, and exclusions. Ask only when a missing choice changes the boundary materially.

### 2. Establish the focal-product baseline

Describe the focal product before searching:

- buyer and ICP;
- core problem and jobs;
- platform, channel, geography, and vertical;
- product form and operating mechanism;
- advice versus real execution;
- one-off use versus monitoring or scheduled work;
- commercial model and plausible replacement decision.

Do not infer the baseline from competitor language.

### 3. Discover candidates broadly

Use the routes relevant to the market: same customer, same job, similar mechanism, platform ecosystem, segment, value chain, alternative path, adjacent entrant, and website reference.

Use directories, listicles, and social posts for discovery only. Confirm important facts through official product pages, documentation, pricing, changelogs, app listings, cases, or hands-on access.

### 4. Classify narrowly

Require all three gates before calling an object a direct competitor:

1. Buyer or ICP materially overlaps.
2. Core job or high-value workflow materially overlaps.
3. A buyer could plausibly choose, adopt, or expand one instead of the other.

Use these default layers:

- **D1 structural direct:** buyer, job, and mechanism strongly overlap.
- **D2 platform-native:** narrower scope with first-party data, distribution, or bundling advantages.
- **D3 segment direct:** highly substitutable in one geography, channel, vertical, or workflow.
- **A adjacent:** useful overlap but a different buyer, job, or purchase decision.
- **S substitute:** reaches the outcome through a different model.
- **R website reference:** useful for communication design, not product competition.
- **W watchlist:** strategically relevant but early or weakly evidenced.

### 5. Maintain an evidence ledger

For every priority object, capture its final layer, buyer, job, mechanism, availability, source URL, access date, evidence status, contradictions, unknowns, and next validation action.

Label evidence as one of: hands-on verified, official public information, official self-description, analysis inference, or unknown. Never convert “not mentioned” into “No.”

### 6. Compare and decide

Compare buyer, job, operating model, data/context, execution depth, continuity, governance, maturity, and commercial replacement. Use written anchors such as Yes / Partial / No / Unknown instead of unanchored scores.

For every priority competitor, conclude:

- why it matters now;
- what it can replace and for whom;
- its strongest structural advantage;
- what remains unverified;
- what to learn, avoid copying, and prove differently.

Lead the deliverable with the decision implication, then show the evidence and limitations.

## Guardrails

- Keep product competitors, substitutes, emerging signals, and website references in separate pools.
- Cite time-sensitive claims next to the conclusion they support.
- Separate availability from strategic similarity.
- Stop expanding the list when new candidates no longer change the map or decision.
- Do not use this skill for a simple factual lookup about one known company.
